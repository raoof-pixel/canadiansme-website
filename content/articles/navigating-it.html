More than 25 years ago, business technology was a very different world. Internet connections crawled, remote-support tools barely existed, and fixing a computer usually meant driving to the client’s office.

Networks were simple: a basic firewall, an unmanaged switch, and a printer that seemed to have a personality of its own. Antivirus updates arrived on diskettes, and passwords like “1234” went unchanged for years. Those memories are amusing, but they show how dramatically the risks have changed and how businesses now completely depend on technology.

Technology became the business

Today, technology is woven into nearly every business function, including communications, finance, sales, customer service, and logistics. Cloud computing lets people work from almost anywhere, and AI can generate content, analyze data, and automate routine work.

But greater connectivity also means greater dependency. When technology stops, the business stops with it. The better question is not “How quickly can we fix the problem?” It is “What could we have done to prevent it?”

AI as business advantage or attack weapon

The same artificial intelligence that is helping businesses grow faster is handing cybercriminals a matching set of tools. Small businesses use AI to draft proposals, analyze data, answer customers after hours, and automate work. Used well, it is a genuine equalizer for smaller Canadian firms.

But the same technology has rewritten the rules of attack. The old giveaways of phishing, such as poor spelling and awkward phrasing, are gone. AI now produces polished, personalized messages that convincingly impersonate an executive, colleague, or supplier.

In early 2024, a finance employee at engineering firm Arup wired roughly US$25.6 million after a video call in which the CFO and every colleague were AI-generated deepfakes. Deepfake incidents in Canada rose by more than 500% between 2023 and 2025.

The risk is not only inbound. Some of the worst exposure comes from inside, when well-meaning employees paste client records, financials, or code into public AI tools. Every organization should set clear rules about which tools are approved and what must never be entered into them.

Cybersecurity is a business responsibility

Cybersecurity is often treated as the IT department’s problem. In reality, it is a business risk requiring leadership, employees, and trusted partners to work together. If a request to change a supplier’s banking details arrives by email, staff should verify it by calling a known contact already on file.

Effective cybersecurity works in layers: multifactor authentication, endpoint protection, email filtering, access controls, employee training, tested backups, continuous monitoring, vulnerability management, and a rehearsed incident-response plan.

Proactive IT is preventive maintenance

Business owners already understand preventive maintenance: vehicles are serviced before the engine fails. Technology deserves the same discipline. Updates get applied on schedule. Backups are not just completed but tested. Access is reviewed as people join, change roles, or leave. Aging equipment is replaced on a planned lifecycle rather than in the middle of a crisis.

People belong on that schedule too. Regular security-awareness training turns employees from the most targeted entry point into a business’s first line of defense.

The cloud still requires management

Moving to the cloud does not make data safe; it shifts the risk to a place many businesses stop monitoring. Providers secure the underlying platform, but identities, permissions, devices, backups, and employee behaviour remain the business’s responsibility. Every business should know exactly what its cloud provider protects, what it does not, and how quickly its most critical information could be recovered.
