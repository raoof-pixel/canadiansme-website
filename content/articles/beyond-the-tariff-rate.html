Canadian importers will face higher tariffs, customs restrictions, and compliance concerns. Tariffs are becoming a minor part of a much larger challenge for Canadian small and medium-sized firms engaged in cross-border commerce.

Changes in customs processes, product classifications, rules of origin, importer responsibilities, and landed charges are making it harder for businesses to move goods between Canada and the United States.

UPS hosted a webinar titled Beyond the Tariff Rate: What US and Canadian Importers Need to Do Next on September 10, 2026. The event covered recent Canada-U.S. trade developments and offered practical advice for firms considering costs, compliance duties, and cross-border operations.

Canada-U.S. trade is entering another critical period

The United States remains Canada’s top merchandise export market. In 2025, the United States received 71.7% of Canada’s merchandise exports, down from 75.9% in 2024. Small enterprises are particularly vulnerable to changes south of the border.

According to Innovation, Science, and Economic Development Canada, SMEs accounted for 73.3% of Canadian firms exporting goods in 2024 and 37.9% of the total value of Canadian goods exports. In 2024, the United States received 79.4 percent of small firms’ export value.

Why Section 338 matters to Canadian businesses

Section 338 of the United States Tariff Act of 1930 authorizes the President to apply extra charges of up to 50% in response to what the administration deems discriminatory treatment of US commerce. In July 2026, the US administration used Section 338 to impose additional 50% tariffs on certain Canadian products, including dairy, alcoholic beverages, and motor vehicles. The extra obligations took effect on August 22.

Canada subsequently announced countermeasures. Effective September 8, 2026, Canada imposed tariffs of 15%, 25%, and 50% on $27.6 billion in U.S. imports. These measures make it even more vital for SMEs to understand precise tariff classification, place of origin, and total landed cost.

Executive Order 14411 raises the compliance bar

On June 3, 2026, the United States released Executive Order 14411, Strengthening Customs Enforcement, which focuses on importer identification, duty collection, forced labour restrictions, country of origin, origin marking, intellectual property, and product safety.

Companies may need to look beyond the simple question, “What tariff applies?” Instead, they should ask:

  • Is the product classified using the correct Harmonized System code?
  • Does the declared country of origin have the documentation to support it?
  • Who is the importer of record?
  • Are the customs values calculated correctly?
  • Is the shipment eligible for preferential treatment or tariff relief?
  • What is the true landed cost after tariffs, levies, taxes, and other expenses?

Landed cost visibility becomes a business strategy

For SMEs with thin margins, an unexpected customs fee can transform a profitable shipment into an unprofitable one. Landed cost visibility is progressively influencing pricing, supplier selection, procurement, and customer experience. Canadian businesses should model various tariff situations before goods reach the border.

What the UPS webinar covered

The session focused on five areas: Section 338 and its two-way influence on Canada-United States commerce; Executive Order 14411 and changing importer expectations; current customs and trade rules; compliance risk management; and options for classification reviews, customs support, and landed cost visibility.

Speakers included Catherine Mellor, Vice President of Global Public Affairs; Jessica Rosen, Senior Director of UPS Trade Advisory Services; Carolyn Kim, Vice President of Public Affairs for UPS Canada; Brian Huh, Product Manager; and Penny Moulton, Director of Trade Management Services and Trade Compliance.

What Canadian SMEs should do now

Importers and exporters can start by analyzing product categories, confirming origin documents, reviewing supplier and customer contracts, modelling landed costs under various tariff scenarios, and identifying where their supply chains still rely heavily on a single market.

For organizations without dedicated customs or trade teams, specialist advice can uncover gaps before they become costly compliance issues. Businesses that understand their risk, have accurate data, and can forecast the impact of a regulation change will be better prepared to respond.
